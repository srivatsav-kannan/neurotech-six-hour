Timing TMS to the brain’s own rhythm

The motor cortex hums at about ten beats a second, a wave called the mu rhythm. Magnetic pulses that land on the wave’s trough have produced stronger effects than pulses at random times. But no one can see where a wave is until it has passed, and every system takes time to fire. So we trained a small neural network to predict the wave ahead of time.

How it works

The signal is scalp EEG over the left motor cortex (electrode C3 minus its four neighbors), where the mu rhythm is strongest. At every moment, the model reads the last second of EEG and predicts where the rhythm will be 0 to 100 milliseconds later. The system fires when the predicted wave reaches its trough, so the pulse arrives on time even after the delay.

The model is a small causal convolutional network with 31,000 parameters. We trained it on six people and tested on the other three, rotating until all nine had been held out. For each test person, one session was used only to calibrate and personalize the model; a separate session recorded on another day was the test. We compared it with the two published real-time methods: autoregressive forecasting, used in the original phase-triggered TMS study, and educated temporal prediction.

To score it, we computed the “true” rhythm afterwards with access to the whole recording, which no real-time system could do. Untimed TMS lands within 45° of the trough 25% of the time by chance.

What would change our mind
  • This is recorded EEG, not a live TMS session. The real test is whether timed pulses produce larger muscle responses or better outcomes.
  • Whether mu phase changes TMS effects at all is debated; one careful study found no effect.3
  • Our model learned toward the same offline definition of the true rhythm that we scored it on; the published methods did not.
  • Nine people from one dataset, recorded during a motor imagery task rather than at rest.

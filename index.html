Question III

From a sentence to a brain scan

In 2018 Pereira and colleagues had people read short sentences in an MRI scanner and recorded what their brains did. A language model can read the same sentences and turn each one into a list of 384 numbers, called an embedding. It turns out that one fixed matrix, W, converts those numbers into the brain activity that was measured. We fit that W ourselves on their public data, for one of the participants.

Why this matters

If one matrix connects a language model to the brain, the model becomes a working stand-in for how we process language. Here is what that makes possible.

Reading a brain by running it backwards

The demo goes from words to a brain scan. The same link works in reverse: a scan becomes a point in the model's space, and the model turns that point into words. This is how the best speech BCIs already work. A language model sits on top of the brain signal and turns noisy recordings into sentences, which is why decoding has improved so quickly and why it keeps improving as models and datasets get bigger.

Running experiments on the model first

A model brain is cheap, so you can try every idea on it and only bring people into a scanner for the few that matter. Tuckute et al. (2024) did exactly this: GPT chose sentences it predicted would switch the language network on or off, and new people in the scanner responded the way it said they would. Human brain studies are slow, expensive and often invasive, which is a big part of why neurotech takes so long to reach patients (question II). Screening on a model first cuts that down.

Brain to brain

Zada et al. (2024) recorded both people in real conversations and found the same embedding appearing in the speaker's brain just before a word and in the listener's brain just after it. So the model's space works as common ground between two people. For someone who can think a sentence but can't say it, their brain activity can be read into that space, a language model can say it out loud, and the other person hears it the normal way and answers.

The risks

Stanford's inner speech decoder sometimes picked up words people were only thinking, such as numbers they were silently counting, and their fix was a mental password the decoder waits for before it switches on. Brain-LLM alignment also follows the model's training data, so a decoder built on an English model works best for English speakers. And these models line up with the brain's language network rather than with thought as a whole.

How we did it

The scans are from the 384-sentence experiment in Pereira et al. (2018), using the language-network voxels packaged by MIT's Brain-Score, for participant 426. The embeddings come from MiniLM, which is small enough to run in your browser, so a typed sentence goes through exactly the same model W was fit on. W is a ridge regression from the 384 numbers to each of the person's 1,357 voxels. Across sentences it was never trained on, its predictions correlate with the measured voxels at a median of 0.16. That is modest in absolute terms, as single-sentence fMRI is noisy, and the examples above are the ones where it matched best. The brain is the MNI152 template, and the order in step 2 follows the published findings rather than this dataset. The code is on GitHub.

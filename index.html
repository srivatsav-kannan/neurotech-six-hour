Which neurotechnology will reach patients first?

For the Neurotech and BCI track, we compared biohybrid implants, connectomics and brain stimulation on six criteria (question IV), tested how language models can decode the brain using a real fMRI dataset (question III), and turned both into what a company should build next.

  1. 1

    Billions of people need better brain treatments, and most neurotech never reaches them.

    Conditions affecting the nervous system were the world’s leading cause of disability in 2021, touching about 3.4 billion people.1 Promising neurotechnology often stalls long before it gets to them.

  2. 2

    We judged three very different fields by how close each is to helping patients, and what stands in the way.

    Biohybrid implants, connectomics and brain stimulation aren’t on the same axis: one is a new interface, one is a mapping tool, one is a therapy already in clinics. We judged them on six criteria borrowed from DARPA, Stanford Biodesign, NASA and the FDA.

  3. 3

    Brain stimulation is furthest along, and the other two fields will mostly reach patients by making it better.

    Stimulation is the only one of the three backed by repeated randomized trials and an adaptive system approved in 2025. Connectomics already helps choose where to stimulate, and biohybrid implants may one day replace the electrode that delivers it.

Question III

Emerging work shows parts of the brain map linearly to LLM activations. How can LLMs improve neural decoding and our understanding of the brain?

From a sentence to a brain scan

Language models and the brain represent sentences in almost the same way. Give a model a sentence and it turns it into 384 numbers. Multiply those numbers by one matrix, W, and you get the brain activity that sentence causes. That single link is why language models can help decode brains and help us study them.

We fit W on real fMRI scans from Pereira et al. (2018), where people read sentences in a scanner. Pick one of the sentences they read to compare W's scan with their real one, or type your own.

What it does for decoding and neuroscience

Because one matrix links the two, a language model can stand in for the brain's language system. That helps in three ways.

Better decoding: run it backwards

The demo goes from words to a brain scan. The same link works in reverse: a scan becomes a point in the model's space, and the model turns that point into words. This is how the best speech BCIs already work. A language model sits on top of the brain signal and turns noisy recordings into sentences, which is why decoding has improved so quickly and why it keeps improving as models and datasets get bigger.

Faster neuroscience: experiment on the model first

A model brain is cheap, so you can try every idea on it and only bring people into a scanner for the few that matter. Tuckute et al. (2024) did exactly this: GPT chose sentences it predicted would switch the language network on or off, and new people in the scanner responded the way it said they would. Human brain studies are slow, expensive and often invasive, which is a big part of why neurotech takes so long to reach patients (question II). Screening on a model first cuts that down.

Communication: brain to brain

Zada et al. (2024) recorded both people in real conversations and found the same embedding appearing in the speaker's brain just before a word and in the listener's brain just after it. So the model's space works as common ground between two people. For someone who can think a sentence but can't say it, their brain activity can be read into that space, a language model can say it out loud, and the other person hears it the normal way and answers.

The risks

Thoughts can leak. Stanford's inner speech decoder sometimes picked up words people were only thinking, such as numbers they were silently counting. Their fix was a mental password the decoder waits for before it switches on, which is close to the first privacy feature ever built for thoughts. Decoders are also starting to transfer between people, so less of your own data is needed to read you. Colorado and California now treat neural data as sensitive and Chile has written neural rights into its constitution, but most places have no rules at all.

The same tool can steer people. If a model can pick the sentences that most strongly drive someone's language network, that is useful for science, and it is also exactly what an ad or a feed would want to optimise for. Feeds tuned for engagement already cause problems, and feeds tuned against measured brain responses would go a step further.

It works better for some people than others. How well a model lines up with the brain follows its training data. An English-trained model fits English speakers' brains best and a Chinese-trained one fits Chinese speakers best, so a decoder built on an English model will work worse for everyone else. Which languages a model learned from becomes a question of who gets a medical device that works.

It is easy to overclaim. Some early results that made models look brain-like turned out to come from simple things like sentence length and position in a passage (Feghhi et al., 2024), and some papers put brain labels on model outputs without using any brain data. These models also line up with the brain's language network rather than with thought as a whole: meaning can be decoded from parts of the brain that have nothing to do with language (Horikawa, 2025).

How we did it

The scans are from the 384-sentence experiment in Pereira et al. (2018), using the language-network voxels packaged by MIT's Brain-Score, for participant 426. The model is MiniLM, which is small enough to run in your browser, so a typed sentence goes through exactly the same model W was fit on. W is a ridge regression from MiniLM's 384 numbers to each of the person's 1,357 voxels. The brain is the MNI152 template, and the order the areas light up in step 2 follows published findings. The code is on GitHub.

What this means for the companies of tomorrow

The companies of tomorrow will win the last mile

Brain stimulation already works for many patients. Most of the value still left on the table sits between a working device and the people who never get it, or never get it tuned well. That gap is a software, data and access problem, and it is where we think new companies can win.

Four openings

Program the device

Software that tunes adaptive stimulation

Adaptive deep brain stimulation, which adjusts itself using brain signals, was approved by the FDA for Parkinson’s in February 2025.1 But programming it is still incompletely defined, and several settings strongly affect performance and take a long time to tune.1,2 In one clinic, only 9 of 20 patients offered it were eligible.2 UCSF is testing an automated pipeline so clinics without specialist expertise can use it.1 That pipeline is a product.

Read the brain

Decoding models as the sensing layer

Closed-loop devices stimulate only when they detect a symptom’s brain signature, like a pacemaker that listens before it acts.3 At UCSF, a device triggered by one patient’s personal depression signal produced a rapid, sustained improvement, though in a single patient.4 Page III shows why language models make this kind of decoding a model and data problem rather than a hardware one.

Reach the patient

Fixing referral and access

Only about 10% of the up to 15% of advanced Parkinson’s patients eligible for DBS are referred to specialized centers.5 Many clinicians don’t realize it is one of the safest neurosurgical procedures and refer too late.3 Women and racial and ethnic minorities are less likely to receive it, even at the same disease severity.6 Tools that find eligible patients and route them to care address a gap the hardware cannot.

Get paid

Making payment move as fast as approval

Novel devices waited a median of 5.7 years after FDA authorization for Medicare coverage, and only 44% reached any coverage in the study period.7 Small companies fared worse: a five-year coverage probability of 19.4%, against 67.9% for large ones.8 CMS has proposed a RAPID pathway that could bring coverage within 60 to 90 days for some breakthrough devices, but it is not final.9

Three tests every founder has to pass

Beat a sham. Deep brain stimulation for depression looked strong in small open-label studies, with about 40 to 50% of patients responding.10 In the sham-controlled BROADEN trial, 20% responded with active stimulation against 17% with sham, and RECLAIM was stopped for futility.11,12 Before believing any next-generation claim, ask whether it has been tested against a sham or an active comparator by someone other than its developers.

Protect the mind. Colorado and California passed the first US state laws treating neural data as sensitive in 2024, and US senators have proposed the MIND Act to study national standards.13,14 As page III notes, Stanford’s inner speech decoder sometimes picked up words people were only thinking, and its fix was a mental password. Privacy has to be designed in, not added later.

Reach everyone. A therapy that only well-referred, well-insured patients get is a smaller market and a worse outcome. The disparities in who receives DBS today are a design problem for tomorrow’s companies.6

The longer bets we would watch

Biohybrid implants are the modality bet from question I. Science Corp, founded by Neuralink’s former president, raised a $230 million Series C, but its first human device will be a sensor without living cells, and its own surgical lead calls trials starting in 2027 optimistic.15,16 We would watch for a two-way interface in a large animal.

Connectomics is infrastructure. Wellcome’s expert group set a whole mouse brain connectome as a 10 to 15 year goal, blocked mainly by the cost of human proofreading.17 We think the first company to make mapping cheap could sell tools to everyone else, the way cheap sequencing did for genomics.

Where this leaves us

Billions of people need better brain treatments. The technology to help many of them already exists, and the next decade’s best companies will be the ones that get it tuned, timed, paid for and delivered, while proving every claim against a sham.
